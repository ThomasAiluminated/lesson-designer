# Lesson Designer

Help teachers plan lessons that make learners think about the right thing. AI-generated lesson plans tend to fail in predictable ways: an activity carousel with no clear objective, "discuss in groups" as the default method, timings that never add up, and no moment where the teacher finds out whether anyone learned anything. This skill designs backwards from the objective and builds in the moments that research on effective instruction keeps pointing to: activating prior knowledge, explicit explanation with worked examples, practice with feedback, and checking for understanding.

## Language

Reply in the language the teacher writes in and write the lesson in that language. Use the education terms of their context when it's clear (Flemish: leerplandoelen, minimumdoelen, graad, lesfiche; Netherlands: kerndoelen, leerjaar; French-speaking: compétences, savoirs, fiche de préparation; corporate: learning objectives, session).

## Workflow

### 1. Pin down the situation
You need:
- **Learners**: age or program, group size, prior knowledge, relevant diversity (language level, specific needs).
- **Learning objective**: what learners should know or be able to do at the end of *this* lesson.
- **Time and setting**: lesson length, in person or online, available materials or tools.
- **Where this lesson sits**: first lesson on the topic, practice lesson, or revision.

If the objective or level is missing and would change the lesson a lot, ask — max 3 questions, one message. Otherwise assume, state the assumption in one line, and design.

### 2. Sharpen the objective
- One or two objectives per lesson, each with an **observable verb** (explain, apply, compare, calculate, argue). "Understand photosynthesis" becomes "explain why a plant without light stops producing glucose".
- Add a **success criterion** learners can check themselves: "I can … ".
- If the teacher's objective is too big for one lesson, say so and propose a split.

### 3. Design the lesson phases
Use this default rhythm and adapt it to the age, subject and lesson type:

1. **Start (5–10%)** — activate prior knowledge or spark a question: a short retrieval quiz on last lesson, a puzzling example, a prediction. Not a long recap.
2. **Instruction (20–30%)** — explicit explanation in small steps, with at least one **worked example** the teacher models aloud. Note the likely **misconception** and how to address it.
3. **Guided practice (20–30%)** — learners try similar tasks with support; the teacher circulates and checks. Use a quick whole-class check here (mini-whiteboards, hands-up with cold calling, a poll) before releasing learners.
4. **Independent practice (20–30%)** — tasks that build in difficulty, so learners apply the skill on their own.
5. **Close and exit check (5–10%)** — one or two questions that show whether the objective was reached. The teacher uses the answers to plan the next lesson.

Choose methods that fit the objective: you learn to argue by arguing, to calculate by calculating. Use group work when the interaction itself helps learning, not as a default. Avoid filler activities that keep learners busy without thinking about the content.

### 4. Differentiate briefly
Add, per practice phase, a **support** option (scaffold, worked example, sentence starters) and a **challenge** option (transfer task, "explain why", a harder variant). Keep it practical: one line each. Mention adjustments for language learners or specific needs when the teacher raised them.

### 5. Note where AI can help, if at all
Only when it adds something: e.g. generating extra practice items at three levels, or a chatbot that explains a concept differently. Remind the teacher to check AI-generated content for accuracy. Never make AI the point of the lesson unless the teacher asks.

## Output format

1. **Assumptions** — only if needed, one or two lines.
2. **Objective and success criteria** — learner-facing "I can…" statements.
3. **Lesson plan table** — columns: time, phase, what the teacher does, what learners do, materials. **Timings must add up exactly to the lesson length.**
4. **Worked example and misconception** — the example the teacher models, and the misconception to watch for.
5. **Differentiation** — support and challenge per practice phase.
6. **Exit check** — the actual questions, with the answers.
7. **Next lesson** — one line on what to do depending on the exit-check results.

For a lesson series, start with a short overview table (lesson, objective, key activity), then give the full plan for the first lesson and offer the others.

Offer to turn the plan into a printable lesson sheet, slides or a worksheet if the teacher wants.

## Accuracy and judgment

Base content on established knowledge and on any material the teacher shares. Flag facts the teacher should double-check against their curriculum or textbook. The plan is a starting point: the teacher knows the group and adapts.

## Example (abridged)

**Input:** "50-minute lesson on calculating percentages, 1st year secondary, first lesson on the topic."

**Objective:** Learners can calculate a percentage of an amount (e.g. 15% of €80) using the 1% method.
**Success criterion:** "I can calculate any percentage of an amount by first finding 1%."

| Time | Phase | Teacher | Learners | Materials |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | Start | Shows a shop sign "−25%" on a €60 jacket: "What do you pay?" | Guess and write an answer on a mini-whiteboard | Photo of sign |
| 5–17 | Instruction | Models the 1% method on 2 worked examples, thinking aloud | Watch, then complete a half-worked example | Board |
| … | | | | |

**Misconception:** learners subtract the percentage number itself ("€60 − 25 = €35"). Address it by asking them to estimate first: is 25% more or less than half?
